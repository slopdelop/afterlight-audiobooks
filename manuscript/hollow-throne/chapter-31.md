# Chapter Thirty-One: The Open Door

The first argument concerned the chairs.

Leia had asked for forty-three of them. The building administrator had provided a curved table, a small platform for speeches and forty-three identical chairs arranged to face it. There were flowers at the center of the table. Someone had placed a Republic emblem on the wall.

Jessa took one look and began moving the furniture.

By the time Leia arrived, the platform was leaning against the corridor wall. The chairs stood in uneven groups around the windows. The flowers had been put beside a woman who wanted to know whether they were real.

"I hope you don't mind," Jessa said.

Leia looked at the abandoned platform.

"I've been trying to get rid of one of those all week."

Three weeks had passed since the palace operation. The forty-three Alderaanians were alive and accounted for, though several remained in hospital and others had chosen quieter places than this crowded reception. Their chairs would be kept available, Jessa explained, but nobody was to be told to sit in one.

Leia agreed.

She had brought a list of practical questions: travel documents, housing, access to their recovered records, the small grants the provisional government had finally approved. The list remained in her bag while people began asking about quite different things.

Was there still a particular kind of bread on Chandrila? Had anybody found a recording of the winter concert from the year before the war? Did the refugee offices really require a place of birth to issue a residence permit when the place no longer existed?

Leia answered the questions she could. For the last, she took out her datapad and made a note she intended to pursue that afternoon.

Teren Daal sat beside an open window, watching a gardener argue with a faulty irrigation line on the terrace below. He had declined the new clothes laid out for him and wore a work shirt with sleeves rolled to the elbow.

"That fitting is wrong," he told Leia when she joined him.

She followed his gaze. "Would you like me to ask him to come up?"

"No. I'd like to go down."

They took the service stair. Halfway to the terrace, he stopped to catch his breath. Leia waited beside him without pretending to look at anything else.

"I used to know every watercourse in my district," he said. "Where it ran. Who needed it. Which families would quarrel if you let one field have it before another."

"Where was that?"

He told her. She knew the valley, though she had never visited it. She had seen it once from the air, a narrow silver thread between fields that seemed too small to feed the towns around them.

"I remember the terraces," she said.

Teren nodded. For a while he leaned against the rail, looking down at the unfamiliar garden.

"I thought there ought to be someone left who knew what that name meant."

Leia put her hand beside his on the rail.

Below them, the gardener swore at the fitting.

Teren straightened.

"I suppose I should tell him."

***

The public report was longer than Leia wanted and shorter than the evidence deserved.

She read its final version in a quiet room overlooking the same terrace. The account traced the laboratories, the prisoners, the forged authorities and the people who had used ordinary rules to conceal extraordinary crimes. It named the dead where their families had consented. It stated clearly where the records failed.

The personal baseline files were not included. Nor were the executable procedures, the calibrated patterns or the detailed instructions for rebuilding the command apparatus. The original evidence remained under independent medical and judicial custody, with access for the people whose lives it described.

There would be objections. Some officials wanted the entire affair buried to preserve confidence in the government. Others wanted every intimate word released so that no one could accuse them of hiding anything.

Leia signed the version that told the truth about the machinery without delivering its victims to another audience.

Venn came in while she was finishing. The commander wore civilian clothes, though the way she stopped inside the doorway suggested she had not quite accepted the change.

"The dispatch hearings begin tomorrow," she said.

"And the technicians?"

"Separate reviews. The medical teams are helping establish who was acting under conditioning. We have witnesses for the rest."

Leia set down the stylus. "Good."

Veyr remained in secure medical custody. The damaged contacts beneath his collar had been removed, and specialists were assessing what he had done to himself. He had given a precise account of several technical matters and refused to acknowledge others. Whatever capacity for doubt returned to him would not erase his choices, or the evidence for them. There would be a trial.

Venn laid a second packet on the desk. It contained the final disposal confirmations.

The Mos Eisley equipment had been removed under municipal observation. The remaining Bespin enrollment instruments were dismantled. On Dagobah, a small team had followed Artoo's recovered survey into the flooded installation, disconnected the surviving transmitter and lifted out the isolated recording apparatus. Its repeated answers had been preserved routines and old responses within the measured pattern, with a separate supervisory channel Veyr had continued using. The team had found no hidden operator and no means for it to address the disabled network after the palace path was cut.

The Endor notary assembly had been retained as inert evidence. The palace's calibrated write bridge, command emitters and black core were in pieces. The diverted signing wafer's serial was revoked in every recovered controller; its destroyed crystal lay in a sealed evidence tray.

Leia read the confirmations to the end.

"Luke will want the Dagobah survey," she said.

"He's already asked."

"Of course he has."

Venn smiled slightly. "He asked whether the workers got out safely first."

Leia closed the packet.

"Of course he did."

***

Sela did not recognize the box.

It was narrow, lined with gray fabric and marked with a new Republic evidence number. She turned it over before opening it, checking the seal and the accession record attached beneath.

The wooden star lay inside.

For a long time she did not touch it.

It had come from the palace's retained teaching collection. The evidence officers had traced its old inventory mark through the Temple laboratory, the black-stone facility and two later transfers. Veyr's staff had kept it because of what it could make a child remember. For years it had rested in a cabinet under a number.

Now somebody had put her name on the receipt.

Iven sat opposite her at the little table. His hair had gone gray at the temples. He had a habit of choosing the chair nearest a wall, and she had learned to ask before moving it.

He was watching her rather than the star.

"Do you want me to put it away?" he asked.

"Not yet."

She lifted it. The wood was lighter than she remembered. One point was blunt where she had dropped it on the Temple steps before any of this began. She ran a finger over the old mark and found herself remembering an afternoon in which nothing important had happened.

Master Varo had been late. Iven had complained. Someone had spilled a cup of water, and they had spent the first part of the lesson moving their cushions away from the puddle.

She laughed.

Iven looked startled.

"The cushions," she said.

He stared at her, then covered his mouth with his hand. After a moment he began to laugh too.

There was a knock at the door.

Sela's shoulders tightened before she could stop them. Iven saw it. He waited until she nodded before he answered.

Nemi stood in the passage, holding a cloth bag so tightly that its handles had cut pale lines across her fingers. She had arrived under the protected name she had used for years. The journey had taken her longer than she intended because she had changed transports twice, then nearly gone home.

Sela stood.

Nobody spoke for a moment. Nemi looked from one of them to the other, taking in the years they had missed. Then she put down the bag and crossed the room.

They held one another awkwardly at first. Iven kept one hand on the table as though he needed to be certain where the room was. Sela felt Nemi's breath catch against her shoulder.

Afterward they sat together, and there were ordinary things to learn. Where Nemi lived. What she did. Whether the person sharing her home knew who she had been. How often Iven wanted visitors. Which foods Sela no longer liked despite having missed them for years.

The other two names lay between them until Nemi took a folded page from her bag.

Mira had died during the first years of the experiments. The medical record named the failure that killed her, though its author had tried to make the language spare enough to hide responsibility. Oren had escaped much later. For nine years he had lived in a coastal town under another name, repaired small engines and kept a room above a shop. He had died in an ordinary accident, with people who knew him beside him. Nemi had brought a letter from one of them.

Sela read it twice.

"Did he have friends?"

"Yes," Nemi said.

"Good."

They agreed that Mira's name should be restored to the public record. Oren's surviving friends would be asked before more of his life was disclosed. Nemi wanted time to decide about her own name. Nobody asked her to decide that afternoon.

When the light began to change, Sela placed the wooden star on the windowsill beside a pot of cuttings the gardener had given her. It could stay there for now.

***

Darren had packed too much.

He knew it when Han picked up the smaller bag and made a face, though Han was polite enough to blame the handle.

"This thing's trying to cut my fingers off."

"I can carry it."

"You can carry the one with the good handle."

They stood on a civilian departure platform above the morning traffic. No military escort waited at the shuttle. Darren had checked that twice.

His first journey would be short. A quiet coastal rehabilitation house on Chandrila had offered him a room, a course of treatment and no duties at all. He had nearly refused because he could not understand what he was expected to provide in return.

Sovan Kell had explained that this was a question he could ask again after sleeping there for a week. Then the medic had written the details down so Darren would not have to depend on remembering the conversation.

Leia came through the gate carrying a small packet.

"Your corrected documents," she said.

He took them. His name appeared without the palace rank or the old subject designation. The citizenship field recognized Alderaan. The residence field had been left for him to complete.

He ran his thumb along the blank line.

"You can keep the rank if you want," Leia said. "This doesn't take anything from you."

"I know."

He folded the packet and put it inside his jacket.

For weeks people had offered him meaningful work. Testimony, advisory positions, a place on the rehabilitation project. Most had meant well. He was grateful to the few who had been able to hear him say no without assuring him he would feel differently later.

"I may come back," he said.

"I'd like that."

"I don't know when."

"Send a message if you want us to meet you."

The shuttle attendant called for passengers. Darren looked toward the open door, then at the crowded sky beyond it.

"Thank you for finding them," he said.

Leia shook her head. "You left us the names."

He considered that, then took his bag from Han.

This time Han let him.

***

Lando's rehabilitation appointment ended with a disagreement about dancing.

The therapist said six more weeks. Lando said that seemed unnecessarily pessimistic. She invited him to lift his left arm above his shoulder. He stopped arguing.

Han found him in the corridor afterward, looking wounded in a way the actual injury had not achieved.

"Bad news?"

"A profound lack of faith in my ability to heal."

"Thought that was what the sling was for."

Lando gave him the look he deserved. Then they walked to the public landing terrace, where Chewbacca was arguing with a cargo loader about the best way to secure a transport cradle.

The cradle held replacement equipment for the clinics. Bespin's contractors had contributed some of it. Lando had obtained the rest by persuading several companies that an early, generous interest in the Republic's health services would be remembered favorably.

He had also paid for a portion himself. He did not mention that until Han found the invoices.

"You could have just told me," Han said.

"And miss the pleasure of watching you read them?"

Chewbacca called them over. His arm had healed enough for the new fur to look untidy around the dressing. He pointed at a mounting bracket, then at the loader's manifest.

Lando studied the difference.

"He's right. That bracket belongs on the other side."

The loader moved to correct it. Chewbacca folded his arms and gave a satisfied rumble.

"I keep telling people to listen to you," Han said.

Chewbacca looked at him.

"Eventually," Han conceded.

***

Luke met Iven in the building's small archive room.

The table between them held maps, a collection of names and several boxes of material recovered from abandoned Jedi sites. Luke had begun making inquiries. He wanted teachers' accounts, children's exercises, the ordinary practices that might not have seemed important enough for the great Temple histories.

Iven had brought a page describing a game Master Varo used to teach concentration. It involved six stones and an argument about who had moved them. Luke suspected the argument had been part of the lesson.

"May I copy this?" he asked.

"That's why I wrote it down."

Luke smiled. "I still wanted to ask."

Iven looked at the boxes.

"Are you going to build another Temple?"

"I don't know yet. Something smaller to begin with. A place where people can learn."

"Children?"

"Some, perhaps. People who want to come, and families who understand what they're agreeing to."

Iven sat for a while without answering.

"I used to think, if I got out, I'd have to finish everything I started. The training. All the things they interrupted."

Luke set the page down.

"You don't owe the Jedi that."

"I know. Sela told me. More forcefully."

They both smiled.

Through the open window came the sound of Threepio instructing somebody in the proper treatment of a historical object. The ceramic spindle had been separated from all calibration equipment and sealed in a passive display case for a small, controlled historical collection. It had no transmitter, emitter or command interface. Threepio was concerned that the label failed to distinguish an excavated focus from the Imperial machinery later built around it.

Artoo replied from somewhere below the sill. His opinion needed no translation.

Luke looked toward the window. The old household droid's final memory record had confirmed a simple duty faithfully performed: preserve the original observations despite later revisions. Its work had survived through a chain of cabinets and manifests, not a will guiding events from beyond the grave. That record would be kept beside the account of the research it had helped preserve.

The cave itself remained on Dagobah. Luke had no plan to turn it into a school, a weapon or a test that somebody had to pass. The first voice that had spoken of the garden was still unknown. He could live with an unanswered question that no longer held anyone captive.

Iven shifted in his chair.

"If I came," he said, "I would want to visit first."

"Of course."

"And if I decided to go?"

Luke looked at him.

"I'd help you find a transport."

Iven nodded. He picked up another blank page and began drawing the arrangement of the six stones.

***

By evening, the chairs had migrated onto the terrace.

Someone had brought food. Jessa had found a way to hang a length of cloth between two railings so that people could sit out of the wind. Teren was discussing drainage with the gardener. Sovan had left the clinic long enough to eat something before anyone could ask him to help them finish theirs.

Leia came out carrying two cups and found Han leaning against the wall beside the door.

"You're late," he said.

"I had to finish something."

"Government?"

"One residence form."

"Big evening."

She gave him a cup and stood beside him. Across the terrace, Sela and Nemi were speaking quietly while Iven listened, occasionally interrupting to correct something. Chewbacca had drawn Artoo into a dispute about the cargo cradle. Lando was telling a story that appeared to require both hands, despite having been told very firmly to use only one.

"We could go somewhere," Han said.

Leia looked at him.

"For a little while," he added. "Someplace nobody's hidden a laboratory."

"You'd have to check the basement."

"I was hoping to avoid basements entirely."

She laughed, and he smiled at the sound.

"All right," she said. "Let's go somewhere."

"You mean that?"

"Yes."

He put his arm around her. She leaned against him and watched the terrace while the first evening lights appeared between the towers.

It would take time. There were names to restore, people to help, habits of fear that would outlast the machines that had cultivated them. Tomorrow she would return to the work. Tonight, a conversation was beginning at the nearest table, and somebody had left room for them.

Han took her empty cup.

Together they went to sit down.
